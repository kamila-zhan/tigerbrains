The positive reframing concept is discussed within TEAM-CBT psychotherapy framework developed by David Burns in his book `Feeling Great`.

It is applied to our negative feelings and thoughts to see what good they have to offer us.

The reason to apply it is to ease the resistance to changes people might have while doing cognitive behavioral therapy.

### Resistance

We are used to viewing emotions and thoughts as only positive or only negative.  

For example, it is hard to see why feeling anxious and thinking `I am a failure` can be positive.   
And what could be the benefits of seeing them as positive?

Swimming in bad feelings and dark thoughts is sometimes tempting and attractive.  
When this happens, trying to switch into positive emotions and thoughts doesn't feel right or helpful.  
 
It may not sound surprising that people in some way love their negative feelings and are attached to them.  
This may explain why when someone or something forces us to change into a positive, the first reaction can be repulsion.  

While being mindful, try to notice whether you are repelling the positive in some sense and trying to wrap yourself and stay with your negative emotions and thoughts.

### Positive reframing

Whether we realise it or not, everything we feel and think has a reason behind it and aims to help us.  

David Burns invites us to answer the following questions:
- What are the advantages or benefits of feeling this way? (anxiety keeps you safe from danger; intense self-criticism pushes you to work harder)
- What does this negative feeling or thought show about your core values? (feeling like a failure proves you care deeply about high standards and doing your job well)

After answering the questions, it should get clear why we do not hurry to get rid of anxiety, fear or anger.  
In its own way, all these emotions and thoughts represent what we strive for and value.  

### Validation & self-support

Finding out the reason behind a negative feeling or thought shows us another perspective, and we feel less ashamed to hold onto them.  
It helps to practice radical acceptance as well and validate what we feel.

Before running into changing everything negative, the negative seeks understanding and wants to be heard.  
It refuses to go away without us seeing what it tries to tell us or protect us from.  

So our duty is to support all emotions and thoughts even if we do not agree or like them.  

As with radical acceptance, it does not mean we will keep everything as it is and surrender to any emotion and thought.

### After reframing

To leave or not the negative in our minds are not the only options available.    

According to TEAM-CBT, we can define the percentage of how much we believe our thoughts and lower it.  

For example, give `I think my work was done awfully` 90% believe value and aim to decrease it to 30%.  
Apply positive reframing and claim that behind this thought hides the pursuit of high quality and the unsatisfactory feelings remind you to be focused and improve the work.  

With acknowledgement of the benefits, the resistance should ease and we can start to find cognitive distortions and test the truthfulness of the thought.  
The cognitive restructuring helps to reach the goal of starting to believe the thought only at 30% or even 0%.  

As a result, with decreased belief percentage, the intensity of sad feelings decreases too.

### What is right with us

David Burns also suggests viewing things like anxiety not as `what is wrong with us`, but as **`what is right with us`**.

Positive reframing is a tool to see what is right with us, even in mental problems.  

Remember not to completely neglect the benefits of negativity, understand and value it, but still apply CBT methods to ease the feelings and stress.
